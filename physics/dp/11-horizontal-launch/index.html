How to use this simulation

  1. Choose the height h of the bench (or cliff) and the launch speed u — the horizontal speed of the ball as it leaves the edge.
  2. Set each value with ▼ and ▲ (hold to go faster; the ↑ ↓ keys or the mouse wheel in the box also work, Shift = ×10), or type a value in the box. ↺ puts it back to its start value. Outside the classroom range the box turns amber ⚠: fine to explore, but the lab can't do this.
  3. Move the pointer over any graph (or tap it): a dashed line shows the values where it cuts each curve, or the values of the nearest data point.
  4. Press ▶ Launch. Ball A rolls off the edge; ball B is dropped from the edge at the same moment.
  5. The strobe shows the ball every 0.1 s (less often for very long flights). The shadows project each image onto the floor (horizontal motion) and onto the wall scale (vertical motion): equal steps along the floor, growing steps down the wall.
  6. The landing mark shows the range R. "Carbon paper" adds a ±1 cm scatter, like a real mark.
  7. Press ● Record. The Data tab plots R against u for the current height; gradient = time of flight.
  8. ◂ 0.1 s / 0.1 s ▸ (or the ← → keys) step the motion back and forward and pause it, so you can discuss each moment: the velocity and acceleration arrows stay on the screen. Graph tools: Area shades the area under a velocity graph (= displacement) or an acceleration graph (= change in velocity) up to that moment and writes what the area is (its name and value) inside it; Tangent draws the tangent there (gradient of a position graph = velocity, of a velocity graph = acceleration).
  9. Work through the Tasks tab. Write explanations on your worksheet.

The physics

  • Treat the two directions separately; they share only the time t.
  • Horizontal: no force, so ax = 0, vx = u (constant), x = ut.
  • Vertical: only the weight, so ay = −g, starting from vy = 0: vy = −gt, h = ½gt².
  • Time of flight t = √(2h/g) — it depends on the height only, not on u.
  • Range R = ut. Landing speed v = √(u² + vy²) at θ = tan⁻¹(|vy|/u) below the horizontal.

Model: no air resistance (see the Air resistance simulation); g = 9.81 (Earth), 1.62 (Moon), 3.71 (Mars) m s⁻². The ball is drawn bigger than to scale; x and y use the same scale, so the shape of the path is true.

Newton's laws

The badge above the scene says which law applies at this moment: 1st law — at rest or moving at a constant velocity, the resultant force is zero (ΣF = 0); 2nd law — accelerating, ΣF = ma. The table under the free-body diagram adds up the forces (ΣF) and compares them with m a (▾ folds it). Tick 3rd-law pairs to see every force with its partner: the same size and type, in the opposite direction, acting on the other body (the Earth, the floor, the string, the fluid…).
